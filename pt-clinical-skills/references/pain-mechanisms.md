# Pain descriptors and reasoning limits

Reviewed adaptation of the exported pain mechanism document, 2026-10-27. Historical discussion catalogs, when supplied, preserve candidate ideas rather than diagnostic rules.

- Nociceptive pain: use this descriptor only with a case-supported hypothesis involving non-neural tissue nociception, while noting uncertainty.
- Neuropathic pain: pain caused by a lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system. Apply the criterion-based grading below to each relevant pain complaint. In Chinese narrative, use 神經病理性疼痛 to distinguish this descriptor from broader neural involvement.
- Nociplastic pain: keep distinct from central sensitization. Do not assign it solely from duration, stress or a diagnostic name. When discussing formal musculoskeletal criteria, check version, population, relevant findings and missing requirements. Do not transfer them automatically to visceral presentations.
- Mixed contributions may be discussed with separate evidence; these descriptors are not mutually exclusive diagnostic boxes.
- Contributing factors is OPEN: physical, loading, health, environmental, contextual, recovery, psychosocial and other supported factors may matter. Do not force them into a preset taxonomy or equate an observation with causation.

Use possible contributions as hypotheses when the evidence permits, not as guaranteed mechanisms. The package's stars are case-support statuses, not formal possible/probable pain-classification grades.

Sources: [IASP Terminology](https://www.iasp-pain.org/resources/terminology/), [Kosek 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33974577/), [Finnerup 2016](https://doi.org/10.1097/j.pain.0000000000000492). Full checks and source limitations are recorded in the development review; they do not establish every catalog example's validity.

## Neuropathic pain: criteria and grading

User-approved extension, 2026-10-27. Apply the NeuPSIG 2016 framework, also described in the IASP 2026 clinical guide. These are clinical certainty grades, separate from this project's hypothesis-support stars.

For each relevant symptom, record A–D as Met, Not met, Not assessed, or Not provided, with the case source and a brief explanation. Resolve contradictory evidence explicitly; distinguish an unassessed criterion from an examined criterion that is not met.

| Criterion | Conditions supporting classification |
| --- | --- |
| A — Relevant history | History supports a clinically plausible lesion or disease affecting the somatosensory nervous system. Identify the suspected condition and its temporal relationship to the pain. A prior confirmed diagnosis is not required at the possible level; a characteristic disease presentation can support suspicion. |
| B — Neuroanatomically plausible pain distribution | The pain distribution is consistent with the suspected peripheral nerve, root, or central somatosensory lesion/disease. Allow individual variation and disease-consistent spread beyond textbook territories; explain the anatomical relationship. |
| C — Concordant sensory signs | Clinical examination demonstrates sensory abnormalities consistent with the suspected lesion/disease and its distribution. Sensory loss to touch, pinprick, temperature or vibration is particularly relevant; condition-appropriate allodynia or hyperalgesia may also support this criterion. Distinguish examined signs from self-reported numbness or tingling. |
| D — Confirmatory evidence | Appropriate diagnostic investigation or direct anatomical/surgical evidence confirms a relevant somatosensory lesion/disease whose location and nature can explain the pain. Examples include relevant neurophysiology, imaging or skin biopsy findings. Identify the actual report and clinical correspondence. |

| Grade | Usual qualifying conditions |
| --- | --- |
| Possible neuropathic pain | A + B. State which history and distribution support the grade. |
| Probable neuropathic pain | A + B + C. Identify the concordant sensory examination findings. |
| Definite neuropathic pain | A + B + C + D. Identify the confirmatory evidence and its correspondence to the complaint. |

Condition-specific exceptions: when sensory signs are difficult to demonstrate but the relevant lesion/disease is confirmed, the framework allows probable neuropathic pain in appropriate cases. Some conditions have specified surrogate signs, such as characteristic triggering in trigeminal neuralgia or the absent body part in phantom pain. Name the condition, cite the applicable exception and explain its use; A + B + D is not an automatic route to definite. Consult the original framework when an exception is needed.

Necessary limits:

- Symptoms such as burning or electric shocks and screening tools such as DN4 or painDETECT support further assessment; the grade rests on the applicable criteria above.
- Positive sensory signs have limited specificity. Interpret their reproducibility, anatomical pattern and clinical context; a positive neurodynamic test is evidence about mechanosensitivity, not an automatic substitute for C or D.
- Definite means the confirmed lesion/disease can explain the pain; other contributors may coexist, and the grade does not prove that all pain is caused by that lesion. Grade individual complaints rather than automatically classifying the entire case.
- Missing criteria leave the grade undetermined or at the highest supported level. State the exact missing criterion and the assessment that could change the grade. Available history and examination can support possible/probable without requiring imaging first.
- This framework grades neuropathic pain, not only chronic neuropathic pain; do not add a universal three-month threshold. Deep somatic/visceral sensory assessment has limitations that can restrict achievable certainty.

## Increased neural mechanosensitivity: clinical finding and hypothesis

Increased neural mechanosensitivity describes a heightened symptom response to mechanical loading of neural structures. Record it as a clinical finding/functional impairment hypothesis, alongside a separate pain-mechanism assessment. It can be clinically supported even when neuropathic pain grading remains undetermined.

For neurodynamic assessment, document:

1. The test, side, symptom location/quality, symptom onset or range, and whether it reproduces the person's familiar complaint rather than only a general stretch sensation.
2. Whether appropriate structural differentiation changes the familiar symptoms: a remote movement alters neural loading while minimizing changes to local symptomatic tissues. Describe the actual maneuver and response.
3. Relevant comparison-side findings, repeatability and clinical context, including competing local tissue explanations. Record missing details rather than supplying assumed findings.

Use the following evidence-linked wording:

| Available evidence | Appropriate interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| Neural-related clues with limited specificity | Possible neural involvement, with the supporting clues and remaining uncertainty. |
| Mechanosensitivity-specific clues with incomplete testing details | Suspected increased neural mechanosensitivity; specify what needs clarification. |
| Familiar symptoms reproduced and modulated by appropriate structural differentiation, with the overall pattern supporting neural loading sensitivity | Findings support increased neural mechanosensitivity; identify the test, response and relevant limitations. |
| Only “ULNT positive” or a similar label supplied | Reported positive neurodynamic test; interpretation limited by missing response details. Request familiar-symptom and structural-differentiation findings. |
| Mechanosensitivity findings plus independently satisfied NeuPSIG criteria | Record both increased neural mechanosensitivity and the supported neuropathic pain grade, each with its own evidence. |

These operational reporting rules are a project adaptation, informed by Schmid et al. (2009), not a new validated diagnostic classification. That study assessed intertester reliability in 31 people with unilateral arm/neck pain using symptom reproduction and structural differentiation for ULNT interpretation. Its reliability findings do not establish diagnostic accuracy or universal thresholds for every nerve, region or test. Range restriction, side differences, stretch sensations or tenderness alone require contextual interpretation. A supported mechanosensitivity finding does not by itself establish a specific lesion, entrapment, inflammation or the exact symptomatic nerve.

Assess neural function and mechanosensitivity separately. Normal routine neurological findings do not by themselves exclude mechanosensitivity; absence of demonstrable mechanosensitivity does not by itself exclude neuropathic pain. When neuropathic criteria remain unresolved, preserve the supported clinical finding and specify the uncertainty in pain classification; assign nociceptive or nociplastic contributions only on their own case evidence.

## Reporting within the existing workflow

- Subjective analysis: identify preliminary hypotheses and available A/B evidence; use supplied examination/report data where available, with provenance. Retain stage boundaries.
- Objective analysis: record mechanosensitivity evidence in Table 3.1 Nerve and Table 3.2 Nervous system, as applicable. Update A–D using available findings.
- Impression: retain the supported functional finding and separately state the neuropathic grade or the exact reason it remains undetermined. Keep Table 4 and the full Impression consistent.
- Place the compact criterion rationale and next discriminating assessment in existing content cells or Reasoning Rationale and Limitations. Preserve fixed table schemas. The next assessment should identify its clinical question, appropriate method/source and how its result would change interpretation.
- Keep stars for support of the particular hypothesis. Strong mechanosensitivity support does not translate into definite neuropathic pain; formal grades do not automatically map to stars.

Example (illustrative, not patient data):

> Findings support increased neural mechanosensitivity: the neurodynamic test reproduced the familiar forearm symptoms, which changed with documented structural differentiation. Neuropathic pain grading remains undetermined: the relevant lesion/disease history (A) and sensory examination (C) were not provided. Clarify the relevant history and pain distribution, then assess sensory function in the suspected territory to update the grade.
