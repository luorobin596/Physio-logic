# Myofascial / fascial system analysis for clinical reasoning

## Purpose and Use

- **Primary use:** Generate anatomically and functionally plausible candidates for each symptom in subjective-analysis Phase 1, Step 3. Explore local, proximal, distal and cross-system relationships; a system explanation need not involve the whole body.
- **Entry clues:** Relevant symptom distribution, movement/load or positional behavior, pulling or tightness, injury/surgery/immobilization history, and relationships with neural, circulatory, lymphatic, head/neck or visceral functions provide starting points for exploration.
- **Candidate scope:** Explain which structures relate, under what circumstances, how the relationship could participate in the symptom, and which regions or questions deserve exploration. Anatomical and functional knowledge supports candidate generation; case relevance can then be clarified through history and examination.
- **Table distinction:** Table 1.1 considers individual adjacent or remote tissue sources. Table 1.2 develops the relationships among structures and functions that could explain or influence the symptom.
- **Framework scope:** Select Anatomy Trains, Fascial Manipulation (FM, Stecco Method), other relevant frameworks or direct anatomical/functional reasoning according to the clinical question. Each framework contributes its own organization of the relationships.
- **Stage boundary:** Preserve symptom IDs and distinguish patient reports, supplied observations, framework knowledge and current inference. Explore shared clues and potential relationships across symptoms; Phase 1 can leave open whether candidates are alternatives, coexist or influence each other. Subsequent objective findings update the proposed links.

Read the relevant sections below: tissue function; four-core workflow; framework anatomy and use; neural, vascular, lymphatic, headache and visceral directions; verification; Table 1.2 return. Select the directions relevant to the case.

## Tissue Function and Possible Symptom Contributions

- **Glide:** Fascial layers and surrounding structures move relative to one another. Loose connective tissue and hyaluronan occupy interfaces between fascial sublayers and between fascia and muscle; their properties are relevant to glide and tissue adaptation during movement. See [Stecco et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21964857/).
- **Tissue change:** Changes in viscosity, stiffness or tissue connections may alter relative movement and tension/load distribution. Stecco uses densification for proposed hyaluronan-related aggregation and glide changes; fibrosis and scar-like adhesions describe other tissue changes. These concepts provide possible explanations for pulling, stiffness or altered movement, without requiring precise pathology identification to begin reasoning. See [Stecco et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35447719/).
- **Pain:** Fascia can participate in nociception; chemical stimulation of thoracolumbar fascia has experimentally evoked pain. A symptom candidate may concern local tissue pain, referred pain, or an influence on another structure's loading or function. See [Schilder et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24076047/).
- **Interfaces:** Tissue tension, glide and available space can be considered in relation to nearby nerves, vessels, lymphatics and viscera. Name the intervening function, such as neural movement, perfusion, drainage or organ/body-wall movement, as developed in the relevant sections below.

## Workflow: Four Core Questions

### 1. Structural Connections

1. **Start:** Preserve the symptom's reported location, distribution and character. Review relevant Table 1.1 sources and the history; a localized tissue candidate is not required before a functional relationship can be considered.
2. **Name structures:** Identify relevant muscles, fascial layers, septa, aponeuroses, retinacula, attachments, scars or organ-associated connective tissues, with the specificity the information permits.
3. **Describe connections:** Explain the specific anatomical continuity, attachment or interface, including relationships to nerves, vessels, lymphatics or viscera when relevant.
4. **Select a map:** If using a named line, unit, sequence, diagonal or spiral, identify the framework and why that organization is useful here. Consult the framework sources for detailed anatomy, routes or point locations as needed.

### 2. Functional Relationships

1. **Situation:** Identify the relevant posture, joint/segment positions, movement direction, task phase, load, repetition or duration. For visceral-related symptoms, include the relevant filling, emptying, respiratory or other functional context.
2. **Proposed relationship:** Explore tension/load distribution, relative glide, movement adaptation, support, pressure/space, or the framework's proposed coordination relationship. Include upstream/downstream structures and pathway direction where relevant; use network, regulatory or other functional relationships when appropriate.
3. **Task participation:** Distinguish moving, controlling, supporting and maintaining position. A whole-task label does not specify every segment's action or every tissue's role.
4. **Individual status:** Relate the proposed task relationship to the available reports and findings, retaining the hypothesis status of changes that await assessment.

### 3. Symptom Connection

1. **Build the link:** State the selected structural relationship → proposed action in the relevant situation → possible connection to this symptom. For a cross-system candidate, name the intervening neural, vascular, lymphatic or organ function.
2. **Explore roles:** Consider possible symptom generation, an influence on load or function, an accompanying/adaptive change, or an unresolved relationship. These are possibilities to describe, not a mandatory classification or ranking.
3. **History basis:** Use direction/position/load dependence, temporal relationships, relevant previous events and persistent changes. A remote region need not itself hurt to be a reasonable candidate; identify when its relevance is based on anatomy/framework alone and needs case clarification.
4. **Keep links explicit:** Distinguish local or referred pain, neural symptom pathways, vascular/lymphatic processes and mechanical effects through connected tissues. Referred pain is not evidence that a pain signal travels down a fascial line.
5. **Shared clues across symptoms:** Compare distribution, provoking directions, load, timing and previous events across symptoms. Explore myofascial or cross-system candidates that could explain several complaints, and describe how each symptom fits the proposed relationship and what remains to be clarified. Keep the symptom IDs available for tracking while developing this more complete explanation.

### 4. Exploration Scope

Use these four steps to choose and update regions:

1. **Establish a relevant route, then select regions:** Anatomical connections, task/function relationships and case clues are separate reasons for inclusion; all three need not already be present. State the reason and missing information for each selected region. Expand from local to relevant proximal/distal or other regions as justified, without an arbitrary distance cutoff.
2. **Use a framework as an exploration map:** Trace relevant lines/sequences or other relationships and select useful nodes/segments. Being on the map makes a region available for consideration, not an established abnormality. Do not automatically list the entire line or exclude an asymptomatic remote node.
3. **Make the exploration useful:** For each region, identify what history or examination could clarify its connection. Include prior injuries, surgery, persistent functional changes and position-dependent symptom behavior where relevant; use existing information without repeating questions.
4. **Adjust with information:** Broaden when task demands, anatomy or new clues suggest additional relationships; focus when some regions better fit the clinical question; change route/framework when the proposed direction no longer fits. Missing remote data remain unknown, not normal or excluded. Prioritizing examination does not remove other reasonable Phase 1 candidates.

## Framework Anatomy and Use

### Anatomy Trains — Thomas Myers

- **Organization:** Myofascial meridians follow anatomical continuity, fiber direction and depth, with bony attachments, branches and transitions helping define the route. The map supports reasoning about how connected tissues share tension and load across regions. See [Myers' organizing principles](https://dl.anatomytrains.com/hnts-fatatw-2.pdf).

#### Selecting Lines and Forming Candidates

1. **Locate candidate routes:** Match symptom distribution, relevant tissue depth and injury/surgery history to possible lines and nodes. More than one route may be relevant to a region.
2. **Place them in the task:** Identify limb/segment positions, where load is applied, and whether the task involves pushing, pulling, gripping, hanging, support or rotation. Analyze which selected connections could participate under those conditions; configuration is part of the functional question as well as line selection.
3. **Reason along the route:** Explore whether activity or tension in one part could alter another node's movement demand, support or loading. Follow proximal or distal connections to useful regions; persistent change after an earlier event can help prioritize a node.
4. **Compare symptoms:** Look for shared position/load responses or timing across connected regions and explain the proposed connection to each complaint.
5. **State the functional candidate:** Describe the selected route, relevant situation and possible effect on the symptom region, then identify the next useful region or question through the four-core workflow.

#### Representative Example: Spiral Line in a Turning Task

The following worked example develops one route in context rather than cataloguing all lines. Its anatomical background includes the upper trunk as well as the lower-limb loop. The line has paired relationships and crosses the midline; follow the actual side at each transition when applying it to a person.

- **Upper route:** Splenius capitis/cervicis — posterior cervical midline — contralateral rhomboids — serratus anterior — external oblique — abdominal fascia/linea alba — opposite internal oblique — iliac crest/TFL.
- **Lower loop and posterior return:** TFL — iliotibial tissues — tibialis anterior — medial cuneiform/first-metatarsal region — fibularis longus — fibular head/biceps femoris — ischial region/sacrotuberous ligament — sacral tissues/spinal extensors toward the skull.

Sources: [Myers' original text, Chapter 6, especially Figures 6.3–6.4 and the lower-leg section](https://f4academy.f4intranet.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/TEMA-1-ANATOMIA-Myers-Vias-anatomicas-Meridianos-miofasciales.pdf) and [Myers' dissection account, Figure 4](https://www.anatomytrains.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/07/dissection.pdf). These links describe the framework's anatomical route; the clinical reasoning below applies that map to an illustrative scenario.

1. **Situation and selection:** Suppose a person reports trunk/shoulder-blade pulling and lower-leg discomfort during a planted-foot turn. Rotation with a fixed support foot makes a cross-body trunk–pelvis–foot relationship worth exploring, bringing the Spiral Line into consideration.
2. **Functional relationship:** Follow the scapular–rib–oblique connections to the pelvis, then the leg/foot loop. Explore how the trunk turns relative to the pelvis, how the supporting foot accommodates the turn, and where movement or tension demands could be redistributed. A perceived restriction at one region can generate a candidate relationship with other participating regions.
3. **Symptom connection:** Compare whether trunk pulling and lower-leg discomfort arise in the same turn direction, stance or loading condition. The shared behavior could support a common or related load-distribution explanation while each symptom remains trackable.
4. **Candidate and exploration:** A candidate can state: “The cross-body trunk–pelvis–foot relationship described by the Spiral Line may contribute to the demands of a planted-foot turn and to the reported trunk and lower-leg symptoms.” Clarify the turn direction, support side, heel/foot behavior, symptom timing and relevant history, then select the corresponding trunk, pelvic and leg nodes for assessment.

#### Extending Beyond the Example

Use existing anatomical and movement knowledge with the selection principles above to generate other relevant routes and candidates. For a named line's exact transitions, side changes, branches or attachments, consult [Myers' original text, Chapters 3–9](https://f4academy.f4intranet.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/TEMA-1-ANATOMIA-Myers-Vias-anatomicas-Meridianos-miofasciales.pdf) or the region-specific author material, such as [The Arm Lines](https://dl.anatomytrains.com/bodyreading1.pdf). This is route lookup to support reasoning, not a requirement for direct research proving every proposed clinical link. Apply the same connection → function → symptom → exploration reasoning to lines or body regions outside this example.

### Fascial Manipulation — Luigi Stecco and Collaborators

#### Units and Directional Organization

- **Myofascial unit:** FM groups muscle fibers and associated fascia by the segment and direction they move. Single-joint and biarticular fibers, fascial continuity and myotendinous expansions link the local unit with other segments.
- **Directions:** AN (antemotion), RE (retromotion), LA (lateromotion), ME (mediomotion), IR (intrarotation) and ER (extrarotation) describe directional organization. Their anatomical actions depend on the segment.

The segment/action correspondence determines how a direction is interpreted. For example, AN-HU denotes glenohumeral flexion, while AN-GE denotes knee extension. See the [Stecco original text's terminology preview](https://img.ibs.it/pdf/9788829928248.pdf).

#### Choosing the Scale of Organization

| Organization | Clinical reasoning question | Selection clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sequence | How do corresponding directional units relate across segments? | Direction-specific movement, loading or symptom clues. |
| Diagonal | How is a trajectory between two spatial planes organized? | Combined directions such as forward-and-outward elevation, prompting ante-lateral relationships. |
| Spiral | How are different or opposing segment directions coordinated in a complex action? | Task-specific combinations of adjacent segment actions and distal/proximal relationships. |

Internal/external rotation also has directional sequences; select the organizational scale from the segment combination, not merely the presence of rotation. See [Stecco's second-level text description](https://staging-piccin.piccin.it/it/physiotherapy/2422-fascial-manipulation-practical-part-second-level-9788829929870.html).

#### CC and CF as Assessment Connections

- **CC, Centre of Coordination:** The model's coordination area within a myofascial unit connects a segment/direction question with a region for fascial assessment. The symptomatic location and this assessment area may differ.
- **CF, Centre of Fusion:** The model's areas for combined directional organization help connect diagonal/spiral questions with relevant assessment regions.

These concepts help select regions from functional clues. Subjective analysis can identify relevant units/segments and later incorporate actual movement and CC/CF palpation descriptions. See the [official FM course organization](https://fascialmanipulation.com/en/educational/fascial-manipulation-fm-courses/).

#### Selecting Relationships and Forming Candidates

1. **Identify actions:** Relate each symptom to actual segment directions, positions and loads. For a whole task such as reaching, identify shoulder-girdle, shoulder, elbow and wrist/hand participation individually.
2. **Find shared directional organization:** Corresponding clues during shoulder flexion, elbow flexion and relevant wrist/hand activity can prompt an upper-limb antemotion candidate. Choose other sequences when their actions fit the presentation.
3. **Select segments:** Use prior injuries, immobilization, surgery and persistent activity changes to select local, proximal, distal and related directional regions for exploration. History is useful here and in Myers' framework.
4. **Choose the scale:** Develop same-sequence relationships for directional patterns; consider diagonals or spirals when combined-plane or differing segment actions better describe the task.
5. **Form a shared or single-symptom candidate:** Explain how the selected muscle/fascial relationships could participate in each symptom, then identify useful questions and assessment regions.

#### Representative Example: Upper-Limb Antemotion

This example connects a directional task pattern to specific units, anatomical links and a candidate explanation.

- **Units and actions:** AN-SC (scapular-girdle forward movement) — AN-HU (glenohumeral flexion) — AN-CU (elbow flexion) — AN-CA (wrist flexion) — AN-DI (finger closure in the author's terminology).
- **Anatomical links:** Pectoralis major → brachial fascia; biceps → bicipital aponeurosis → antebrachial fascia; relevant forearm muscles → antebrachial fascia. These connections help explain the model's cross-segment tension and coordination organization.

Sources: [Stecco's terminology preview](https://img.ibs.it/pdf/9788829928248.pdf) and [author framework chapter, upper-limb antemotion sequence](https://elsevier-elibrary.com/contents/fullcontent/58608/epubcontent_v2/OEBPS/xhtml/B9780702034251000076.htm).

1. **Situation and selection:** Suppose a person reports shoulder pain when lifting the arm forward, anterior-elbow pulling during resisted elbow flexion and forearm discomfort with wrist flexion/gripping. These corresponding directional/load clues make an upper-limb antemotion relationship worth exploring.
2. **Functional relationship:** Use the actual segment actions to select relevant units from the sequence. The fascial expansions provide possible connections through which local tissue behavior could relate to other segments' adaptation or demands. Prior injury, immobilization or persistent activity changes can help identify useful proximal/distal regions.
3. **Symptom connection:** Compare the load, timing and direction associated with each complaint. A shared sequence may offer a more complete explanation of the shoulder, elbow and forearm features; identify how each selected unit or connection might participate.
4. **Candidate and exploration:** A candidate can state: “Directional and load-related clues across the shoulder, elbow and wrist/hand suggest exploring an upper-limb antemotion relationship, including how the connected muscle/fascial tissues adapt during loading.” Clarify the relevant actions and symptom responses; examine selected segments and, when appropriate, incorporate actual CC palpation findings. If combined-plane or opposing segment actions better describe the symptoms, use the organization principles above to explore a diagonal or spiral instead.

#### Extending Beyond the Example

Apply segment → direction → relationship scale → anatomical connection → symptom reasoning to other limbs, trunk or complex tasks. Use existing anatomy/movement knowledge to propose candidates, and consult the [author framework chapter](https://elsevier-elibrary.com/contents/fullcontent/58608/epubcontent_v2/OEBPS/xhtml/B9780702034251000076.htm), [original terminology](https://img.ibs.it/pdf/9788829928248.pdf) and [official course organization](https://fascialmanipulation.com/en/educational/fascial-manipulation-fm-courses/) when exact unit actions, sequence membership or CC/CF locations are needed.

FM's organ-fascial units and apparatus fascial sequences/catenaries extend the model to internal functions, as outlined in the official course description. Select the specific organ/function and connections using the visceral section below. Both frameworks supply anatomical and functional maps; overlapping regions can yield complementary candidate explanations while retaining each map's organization.

## Relevant Symptom and Cross-System Directions

Select from these directions, expanding beyond them when relevant anatomy/function suggests another candidate. They are not mandatory diagnoses or requirements to implicate every linked system.

### Neural Interfaces and Numbness

- **Candidate:** Explore how fascia, aponeuroses, retinacula, scars or other surrounding tissue could affect a named nerve's mechanical interface, movement or local pressure. Trace the relevant neural distribution and interface before proposing the symptom link.
- **Example:** Small-finger/ulnar ring-finger tingling aggravated by sustained elbow flexion can prompt exploration of the ulnar nerve and surrounding elbow tissues. Relevant interfaces include the cubital-tunnel roof and other history/location-supported structures; the [ulnar neuropathy guideline](https://seguridad.lni.wa.gov/patient-care/treating-patients/treatment-guidelines-and-resources/_docs/UlnarNerve_2015_Updated_0623.pdf) identifies anatomical candidates. No precise tissue pathology is required before exploring the relationship.
- **Follow-up:** Clarify what “numb” means, exact distribution, onset/recovery with positions, local pressure, old injury/surgery and any persistent sensory or motor change. Extend along relevant nerve and fascial routes where there is a reason.
- **Responsibilities:** The fascial candidate describes the interface; the [nervous-system guide](nervous-system-analysis.md) addresses neural pathways, function and processes. Preserve other neural sites and alternatives. Pain classification and mechanosensitivity remain distinct under the [pain-mechanism guide](pain-mechanisms.md); tingling alone is not neuropathic pain.

### Vascular Interfaces and Circulatory Function

- **Candidate:** Consider surrounding muscle/fascia, fibrous structures, scars and position-dependent space in relation to a relevant vessel. Explore arterial supply or venous return as appropriate, retaining upstream/downstream routes and direction when useful.
- **Example directions:** Exertional calf pain may justify a local tissue–vessel relationship alongside other explanations. Post-injury swelling with reduced ankle movement can raise both a regional interface candidate and an activity/muscle-pump contribution to return. Explain the proposed fascial participation rather than treating every movement deficit as fascial.
- **Follow-up:** Compare activity versus static posture, stopping versus changing position, onset/recovery, limb elevation, temperature/color changes and related history. The vascular process must be stated, not merely “fascia affects circulation.”
- **Anatomical examples:** A [popliteal arterial case series](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7482155/) and [venous fibrous-band case report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16222391/) illustrate specific external interfaces. They justify considering such relationships without making those diagnoses routine candidates. [NHS venous guidance](https://www.guysandstthomas.nhs.uk/health-information/post-thrombotic-syndrome-and-chronic-venous-insufficiency/improving-circulation) describes muscle-pump/ankle function. Use the [vascular guide](vascular-analysis.md) for circulatory processes, assessment and priority.

### Lymphatic Transport and Tissue-Fluid Relationships

- **Candidate:** Explore how regional fascia, subcutaneous/interstitial tissues, tissue pressure and movement could relate to fluid entry and lymphatic transport. Consider tissue/fluid changes influencing glide and activity in the opposite direction as well.
- **Functional link:** Initial lymphatics interact mechanically with the surrounding matrix; increased tissue tension is not uniformly an obstruction. Describe the proposed condition and effect instead of automatically asserting “tight fascia blocks lymph.” See [Schmid-Schönbein, 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2252623/).
- **Example:** Postoperative regional tissue/activity change could participate in transport and swelling/tightness; swelling could also affect tissue movement. Ask about territory, daily course, movement/elevation responses, treatment history and the timing of tissue versus symptom changes.
- **Responsibilities:** Connect the proposed fascial relationship with transport capacity, tissue-fluid load and relevant drainage regions under the [lymphatic guide](lymphatic-analysis.md). Do not equate swelling with primary lymphatic disease or require a causal sequence during Phase 1.

### Headache and Head/Neck Relationships

- **Directions:** Consider local/referred myofascial pain, nerve interfaces, regional support/load relationships, and accompanying/adaptive tension. For example, occipital pain can prompt head/neck tissue candidates and an occipital nerve interface; the greater occipital nerve's relationships to semispinalis capitis and trapezius aponeurotic tissues are described in an [anatomical study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37777340/).
- **Regional scope:** Use headache distribution and task behavior to select head/neck, shoulder-girdle/thoracic or jaw-related regions. Explain any distant framework-based extension. Referred headache and mechanical transmission through connected tissues are different proposed relationships.
- **Follow-up:** Record headache onset/distribution, frequency/duration, familiar versus changed pattern; whether neck/shoulder tension precedes, accompanies or follows it; movement, sustained posture and upper-limb demands. Jaw-function-related temporal pain can prompt masticatory/TMD candidates. Also clarify nausea, light/sound sensitivity, visual/neurological features and medication context rather than assuming all neck-associated headache is fascial.
- **Classification:** Neck movement or pressure provoking headache supports exploration but is not unique to a diagnosis. [ICHD-3 cervicogenic headache](https://ichd-3.org/11-headache-or-facial-pain-attributed-to-disorder-of-the-cranium-neck-eyes-ears-nose-sinuses-teeth-mouth-or-other-facial-or-cervical-structure/11-2-headache-attributed-to-disorder-of-the-neck/11-2-1-cervicogenic-headache/) discusses these limits and the appendix status of cervical myofascial headache. Use [ICHD-3 TMD guidance](https://ichd-3.org/11-headache-or-facial-pain-attributed-to-disorder-of-the-cranium-neck-eyes-ears-nose-sinuses-teeth-mouth-or-other-facial-or-cervical-structure/11-7-headache-attributed-to-temporomandibular-disorder-tmd/) when relevant; Phase 1 candidates need not establish a formal headache diagnosis.

### Visceral Fascia and Organ-Related Functions

- **Scope:** Explore specific organs and their connective-tissue coverings, supports, attachments and interfaces with body wall or other systems. Relate anatomy to support, relative movement, pressure or neural/vascular/lymphatic relationships. Organ-associated fasciae vary by site; see [Stecco et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28466969/).
- **Functional selection:** Use breathing, trunk posture/movement, meals, filling, defecation or voiding relationships as appropriate. Surgery is one clue, not a prerequisite; organ dysfunction or pain may also influence surrounding tissue tension, breathing and movement.
- **Example:** After lower abdominal surgery, trunk-extension pulling can prompt abdominal-wall–surgical-region–organ-interface candidates. If bloating is also reported, compare timing, posture and organ-function clues to explore a shared or related process, explaining the connection to each symptom while retaining their IDs. Without surgery, filling-related discomfort with positional variation can still prompt organ/support-tissue–body-wall or pelvic-floor relationships.
- **FM use:** Where useful, identify the relevant organ-fascial unit or apparatus fascial sequence/catenary, its case basis and selected regions. These are the author's organizing concepts, not automatic diagnoses of digestive, urinary or autonomic dysfunction.
- **Follow-up and responsibilities:** Clarify the actual organ/procedure, tissue depth if known, onset/course and functional timing. Keep individual visceral sources in Table 1.1 and organ/disease/regulatory candidates in their appropriate systems. Mechanical fascial relationships, visceral referral and autonomic regulation are distinct proposed links; use the [autonomic guide](autonomic-nervous-system-analysis.md) for a specific regulatory candidate. [NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/abdominal-adhesions) describes clinically recognized abdominal adhesions, many asymptomatic; use this as a possible context rather than a prerequisite or default explanation.

## Verification Directions and Later Hypothesis Updates

In subjective analysis, specify the question and information that could update each proposed link. When actual examination data arrive, record the familiar symptom, precise region/direction, task/load, timing and comparison conditions. Distinguish the following evidence types:

- **Movement/function:** Range, direction, support and load-related findings can establish a task limitation or symptom relationship; identify the additional information needed to attribute it to selected tissues.
- **Palpation/tissue movement:** Record tenderness, familiar versus unfamiliar symptom reproduction, examiner-perceived resistance and relative movement. Retain useful regional findings without requiring exact histopathology or relabeling every hard/thick area as densification.
- **Local/remote position changes:** Reproducible symptom or function changes can support a regional mechanical relationship. Compare relevant neural, joint, muscular and other explanations before attributing the response to one fascial route.
- **Imaging/measurement:** Interpret morphology, thickness or glide measurements for the actual site, method and conditions. A measurable difference and its symptom contribution are different questions; imaging is not mandatory to generate a candidate.
- **Intervention/retest:** Describe what changed and under which conditions. Response alone does not identify a molecular mechanism or prove an entire line/sequence abnormal; select further evidence according to the unresolved link, without prescribing treatment here.

Update the particular claim rather than assigning one verdict to an entire framework:

1. **Increase support:** Corresponding regional, directional and task findings converge with the familiar symptom.
2. **Narrow:** Retain a local finding when only local evidence is supported; leave the proposed cross-region or symptom connection unresolved.
3. **Weaken:** An appropriate, repeatable assessment fails to show the predicted relationship or provides opposing evidence; describe the conditions and limits.
4. **Redirect:** Other tissue/system findings better explain the complaint or the chosen route no longer fits.
5. **Remain unknown:** Missing, unsuitable or non-symptom-reproducing assessment is not by itself exclusion.

For Myers, update selected nodes and their task/load relationships. For FM, compare directional and segment findings with model-based palpation descriptions. Across symptoms, assess whether proposed shared relationships correspond to each complaint and refine the overall explanation as data accumulate.

## Return to Table 1.2 and Stage Continuity

- **System / Category:** Retain Myofascial / Fascial system.
- **Explanation & Basis:** Summarize the candidate relationship, possible symptom connection, case/anatomical or framework basis and material uncertainty.
- **Structures & Relationships:** Identify selected structures, connections/interfaces, functional conditions and relevant regions; include directional or upstream/downstream analysis when the candidate calls for it.

Keep the summary understandable on its own. When more detail is useful, follow the subjective skill's existing optional system-expansion rules and organize reasoning around the four core questions. This guide defines analytical content, not a new fixed expansion template or a requirement to expand every framework/system.

Objective analysis can use this knowledge to evaluate supplied findings in its existing tables; Impression integrates the supported explanation. Follow the [clinical contract](clinical-contract.md) for stage-specific labels, symptom continuity and mapping the subjective category to the existing objective Myofascial lines row. Cross-system candidates can describe different parts of one proposed process without declaring every connected system abnormal.

## Medical-Assessment Priority

Apply the clinical contract immediately, without waiting for fascial tables or framework testing. Examples relevant to these directions include:

- **Neural:** Progressive weakness, atrophy or other severe/progressive neurological findings warrant prompt medical assessment; use the nervous-system guide for presentation-specific priority.
- **Circulatory:** Sudden painful/cold limb with new sensory or motor change requires emergency assessment; new unexplained unilateral swelling/pain requires timely assessment for possible thrombosis under the vascular guide.
- **Headache:** Sudden severe headache, new neurological deficit or altered consciousness requires urgent/emergency assessment; substantial pattern changes and other concerning features need appropriate investigation/referral. See [NICE CG150](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg150/chapter/recommendations).
- **Abdominal:** Pain with vomiting, marked distension and inability to pass gas/stool raises possible obstruction and needs immediate medical assessment, as described by NIDDK above.
