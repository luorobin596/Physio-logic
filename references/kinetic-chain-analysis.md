# Kinetic-chain analysis for clinical reasoning

## Overview

- **Purpose:** Analyze how body regions and joints coordinate during a functional task, then relate that analysis to reported symptoms and available examination findings.
- **Kinetic chain:** Consider linked movement, support, force and moment transfer, energy generation and absorption across the musculoskeletal system. A region may contribute by maintaining position or controlling motion as well as by moving.
- **Clinical scope:** Use clinically available descriptions, observations and tests. Precise peak-velocity sequencing, timing, power and energy measurements are not required; do not infer them from ordinary observation.

## When to Use

- **Task relevance:** Read this guide when a reported functional or sporting movement makes kinetic-chain reasoning relevant to the current assessment.
- **Available context:** Use relevant history, objective findings and prior subjective/objective outputs available in the current context or handoff. Distinguish reported behavior, observed movement, reference-based task demands and current inference.
- **Stage depth:** Apply only the level supported by the current stage and available data. A literature description of a task is not an observation of this patient.

## Workflow

### Step 1: Task Scope and Phases

1. **Task definition:** Identify the exact task, side, starting position, direction, relevant load or equipment and symptom IDs. Identify missing details that would materially change the analysis.
2. **Analysis boundary:** Determine whether the task is a discrete action or a repeating cycle, and whether the requested analysis covers one direction or the outward-and-return movement.
   - These descriptions can overlap; a discrete action can include preparation, acceleration and deceleration.
   - Examples include a throw or racket swing, a gait or swimming cycle, and forward bending with or without return to standing.
3. **Phase division:** Divide the task into meaningful phases using observable boundary events. Use a relevant established scheme when available and identify its source; search when needed rather than inventing a standard.
   - Select the number of phases for the task and clinical question rather than imposing a fixed count.
   - For a cycle, identify the cycle boundary and side; for a return movement, distinguish the outward and return portions.
   - If patient-specific phase information is unavailable, label a proposed division as reference-based or provisional and ask where symptoms occur.

### Step 2: Regions, Joints and Demands

1. **Participation map:** List the body regions and joints participating in the task across phases, including relevant upstream and downstream regions relative to the symptom location.
2. **Anatomical description:** Describe each participating joint's principal movement or position-control demand in each phase using anatomical movement terms and sagittal, frontal, transverse or combined planes as appropriate.
   - Specify the moving segment and reference segment when needed to make the description clear.
   - Include stabilization or resistance to movement when the joint's role is to maintain position; do not require visible movement in every phase.
3. **Task roles:** Identify relevant support, propulsion, load-transfer or deceleration roles. Separate typical task demands from what was actually observed in the patient.

### Step 3: Symptoms and Clinical Sequence

1. **Symptom mapping:** Place each reported symptom ID within the task phases, including onset, aggravation, relief or persistence where supplied. Keep unknown phase information explicit.
2. **Observable sequence:** Describe clinically observable initiation, relative delay, coordination, movement dissociation and compensatory patterns where data support them.
   - A pelvis–thorax separation can be described as relative motion or delayed participation; do not assume that the thorax is completely stationary.
   - Do not derive elastic-energy storage, joint loading or a peak-velocity sequence from appearance alone.
3. **Regional candidates:** Identify upstream or downstream regions whose task demands, restrictions or control could plausibly relate to the symptom phase. State the basis and uncertainty without treating the chain itself as proof of a symptom source.

### Step 4: Clinical Interpretation

1. **Context integration:** Relate symptom timing and movement descriptions to the detailed history, examination findings and prior analysis available in the current context. Preserve the distinction between a task-demand hypothesis and a demonstrated impairment.
2. **Reasoning models:** Consider relevant movement-control models, including relative flexibility/stiffness and restriction–compensation relationships described below. These are candidate explanations, not mandatory findings or an exhaustive set of mechanisms.
3. **Evidence comparison:** When examination data are available, identify supporting, opposing and missing findings, and the additional assessment that could change the explanation. A movement variation or symptom association alone does not establish causation.
4. **Stage return:** Return the relevant findings or hypotheses to the current skill's existing table fields and reasoning sections. Preserve fixed schemas, symptom-ID continuity and existing stage stops; this guide does not require a separate report for every task.

## Kinetic Control Concepts

### Relative Flexibility and Stiffness

- **Relative behavior:** Consider whether one region or joint moves more readily while another is relatively restricted during the same task or direction. The comparison concerns their relationship, not simply whether one has a large absolute range.
- **Restriction and compensation:** A restriction may be accompanied by additional motion elsewhere to maintain function. Assess the proposed restriction and the ability to control the compensating movement before describing UCM as a supported finding.
- **Clinical connection:** Identify the site, direction and relevant task or test conditions, relate them to symptoms and record uncertainty. Compensation or greater motion alone is not sufficient to establish uncontrolled movement.

### Movement Form

- **Uncontrolled translation:** Refers to control of accessory or translational motion, including intersegmental translation. Identify the joint or motion segment, direction and assessment basis when available.
- **Uncontrolled range:** Refers to control of physiological or functional movement range at one or more segments.
- **Assessment limits:** General movement observation does not establish intra-articular or intersegmental translation. Without relevant examination evidence, retain a hypothesis and identify what requires assessment.

### Restriction–Compensation Relationships

- **Classification basis:** The 2012 book's Chapter 3, Table 3.2 describes the following three relationships. These classify where restriction and compensation relate to one another; they are a different descriptive axis from translation versus range.
- **Intra-articular UCM:** Restriction and uncontrolled movement occur within the same joint; restricted translation in one direction may accompany uncontrolled translation in another direction.
- **Interarticular UCM:** Restriction at one joint is accompanied by compensatory uncontrolled motion at an adjacent joint. Adjacent spinal motion segments are one application of this relationship.
- **Regional UCM:** Restriction and compensatory movement occur between regions, involving physiological/functional range and relative flexibility/stiffness relationships.
- **Intersegmental terminology:** Intersegmental translation describes motion between segments; it is not a fourth peer category in that three-part table.
- **Model status:** Use these relationships as clinical reasoning models and report the evidence for their application. The terminology was checked against the 2012 text; the exact taxonomy has not been checked against the revised edition.

## Use Across Skills

- **Subjective analysis:** Use the reported task, symptom phase and relevant history to expand candidate upstream/downstream regions in Table 1.2. Keep patient-specific movement patterns and UCM unconfirmed when no observation or examination supports them. Phase 1 remains symptom-based source exploration.
- **Objective analysis:** Use supplied observations and tests to examine the proposed restrictions, control patterns and symptom relationships. Place supported structural findings in Table 3.1 and regional/system involvement in Table 3.2, with the clinical contract's status and rationale.
- **Impression:** Integrate the supported movement-related explanation, functional impact, contributing regions and unresolved questions in Table 4 and the selected narrative. Do not introduce unobserved mechanics or assume tests occurred.

## Verification

- [ ] Task boundaries and phase divisions fit the movement and question; reference-based demands are separate from patient findings.
- [ ] Participating regions/joints, anatomical movements or stabilization demands and relevant planes are described across phases.
- [ ] Symptom IDs are preserved and linked to reported phases; missing information remains explicit.
- [ ] Sequence analysis uses available clinical observations rather than requiring precise peak-velocity or energy data.
- [ ] Translation/range descriptions and restriction–compensation relationships are kept distinct, with an assessment basis or explicit hypothesis status.
- [ ] Conclusions match the current skill, available data, fixed schemas and stage stops.

## Sources and Limits

- **Author framework:** [Comerford and Mottram, 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11243904/) describes segmental/articular and multisegmental/myofascial dysfunction, restrictions, compensation and local/global control. It is a clinical framework, not proof of an individual patient's causal mechanism.
- **Taxonomy source:** Comerford and Mottram, *Kinetic Control: The Management of Uncontrolled Movement*, 2012, Chapter 3, Table 3.2, pp. 51–52. Terminology was checked through an [online transcription of the original text](https://studylib.net/doc/28512040/kinetic-control-the-management-of-uncontrolled-movement1); this is not a publisher-hosted edition and may contain transcription errors. Verify against the published book when exact wording or a protocol is needed.
- **Revised framework:** The [publisher's revised-edition description](https://www.us.elsevierhealth.com/kinetic-control-revised-edition-9780729543262.html) identifies site, direction and threshold of UCM alongside pain-sensitive tissues, pain mechanisms and contextual factors; it does not verify every older classification or test procedure.
- **Biomechanics example:** [Aguinaldo and Escamilla, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6390228/) studies segmental motion, energy flow and elbow loading in baseball pitchers using instrumented analysis. Its measurements are context for the model, not required clinical inputs or findings transferable to every task or patient.
