# Autonomic nervous system analysis for clinical reasoning

## Purpose and Scope

- **Purpose:** Explore how autonomic functions and their relationships with other systems could explain or contribute to each symptom.
- **Table distinction:** In Table 1.2, explore autonomic functions, pathways and regulatory relationships that could explain, amplify or otherwise influence the symptom. Extend beyond local tissue-source reasoning where relevant.
- **Selection:** Use the directions below as reasoning prompts. Select those relevant to the presentation and expand them when another autonomic function provides a plausible explanation.
- **Stage depth:** Subjective information supports candidate explanations. Available observations, measurements and reports subsequently update those explanations.
- **Contribution:** Autonomic involvement may concern symptom generation, symptom modulation, functional tolerance or an accompanying response. State the proposed relationship.

## Define the Symptom and Relevant Context

1. **Symptom scope:** Preserve the symptom ID, reported distribution, quality, onset/course and functional impact.
2. **Description:** Clarify what the person means by terms such as coldness, heat, weakness, swelling or dizziness. Distinguish a perceived sensation from a reported external change and an examined finding.
3. **Behavior:** Identify relevant relationships to activity, posture, environmental conditions, eating, elimination, sleep, pain and emotional context, including onset and recovery.
4. **Relevant background:** Consider supplied neurological, cardiovascular, metabolic, visceral, surgical and medication history where it changes the proposed explanation.
5. **Existing candidates:** Review relevant Table 1.1 candidates and consider how autonomic functions might connect them to the symptom. Autonomic reasoning may also arise from symptom behavior or functional context without a localized tissue candidate.

## Select Relevant Explanatory Directions

### Regional Blood Flow and Vasomotor Regulation

- Consider whether autonomic vascular regulation could influence regional blood flow and contribute to symptoms in skin, muscle or neural tissue.
- Relate relevant autonomic pathways and vascular responses to the affected region. Distinguish the observed or reported vascular phenomenon from its proposed autonomic mechanism.
- Consider physiological context and local vascular responsiveness when interpreting vasoconstriction or vasodilation.

- **Possible symptoms and findings:** Coldness or warmth, color changes, pain, numbness, tingling, fatigue or activity-related discomfort.
- **History pattern:** Explore distribution, triggers, episode sequence and recovery, including how sensory symptoms relate to temperature or color changes.
- **System relationship:** Use Vessels to examine perfusion and vascular processes, ANS to examine the regulatory contribution, and Nervous system to examine neural function. Consider whether altered blood supply to a nerve could contribute to sensory symptoms, while evaluating the proposed autonomic link separately.

Sources: [Kellogg et al., 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7586235/) supports physiological context in cutaneous vasodilation; [Stöhr, 1981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7276972/) supports effects of ischemia on human sensory axonal excitability and conduction. [Hogikyan et al., 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10206444/) found different nerve-function responses to adrenergic vasoconstriction between its small diabetic and control groups. These findings support mechanistic consideration, not a universal autonomic explanation for numbness.

### Sweating and Thermoregulatory Function

- Consider whether sweating and heat-dissipation responses could contribute to local symptoms, environmental intolerance or activity limitations.
- Relate central thermoregulatory control, sympathetic sudomotor pathways and sweat-gland responses to the symptom's distribution and thermal or activity demands.
- Interpret sweating and vascular responses as related but distinct components of thermoregulation.

- **Possible symptoms and findings:** Increased, reduced or uneven sweating, perceived wetness or dryness, heat intolerance and symptoms developing in warm environments or during activity.
- **History pattern:** Explore regional versus generalized changes, resting and activity conditions, environmental exposure and recovery.
- **Functional contribution:** Consider whether heat regulation could influence tolerated activity, duration or environmental participation.

Source: [Architecture of the Cutaneous Autonomic Nervous System, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31551921/) describes cutaneous autonomic fibers and effectors.

### Cardiovascular Regulation During Posture, Activity and Recovery

- Consider whether autonomic cardiovascular adjustment could contribute to symptoms during standing, exertion or recovery.
- Relate the proposed contribution to changes in venous return, cardiac output and vascular resistance, including the autonomic reflexes that help maintain circulation.
- Distinguish a potentially inadequate regulatory response from an appropriate response to another circulatory demand or disturbance.
- Cardiovascular autonomic responses should be interpreted through their relationship to the complaint and physiological conditions. [AAS/AANEM consensus, 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10286-026-01238-9)

- **Possible symptoms and findings:** Limited standing or activity tolerance, lightheadedness, near-fainting, palpitations, perceived weakness or fatigue, and relevant heart-rate or blood-pressure changes.
- **History pattern:** Clarify the exact activity or positional transition, symptom timing, duration and recovery. Distinguish stopping exertion from changing posture when interpreting relief.
- **System relationship:** Consider cardiac, vascular, fluid-volume, medication and other relevant explanations alongside autonomic adjustment.

### Gastrointestinal Motility, Secretion and Digestive Regulation

- Consider whether gastrointestinal motility, secretion or digestive regulation could explain or contribute to the symptoms.
- Relate the proposed process to the relevant organ, extrinsic autonomic pathways, enteric circuits and symptom region.
- Digestive regulation involves interactions among neural, hormonal and local mechanisms. Use this relationship to select a plausible explanation rather than attributing digestive symptoms globally to sympathetic excess or reduced vagal activity. [NIDDK digestive physiology](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/digestive-system-how-it-works)

- **Possible symptoms and findings:** Abdominal discomfort, fullness, bloating, nausea, early satiety and changes in bowel function.
- **History pattern:** Explore relationships to meals, fasting, defecation, activity and symptom development/recovery. Clarify the relationship between digestive and musculoskeletal complaints.
- **System relationship:** Distinguish visceral symptom generation, referred symptoms, altered organ function and accompanying autonomic responses. Consider local organ, enteric, endocrine and other explanations.
- **Functional contribution:** Consider how digestive symptoms or altered intake may affect activity and participation where supported by the case.

### Metabolic and Energy Regulation

- Consider whether autonomic–endocrine coordination of fuel availability and energy use could contribute to symptoms or functional limitations.
- Relate the proposed contribution to relevant processes, such as pancreatic hormone secretion, hepatic glucose regulation or mobilization of energy stores.
- Metabolic regulation and autonomic responses can influence one another. Distinguish a proposed regulatory contribution from an autonomic response to a metabolic change.
- Human research supports brain–periphery metabolic relationships, while the effects depend on physiological conditions and population. Use this as a basis for exploration rather than a direct explanation of an individual's fatigue. [Heni et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25028522/), [human experimental study, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30552787/)

- **Possible symptoms and findings:** Fatigue, perceived weakness, reduced activity tolerance or episodic symptoms associated with eating, fasting or exertion.
- **History pattern:** Explore food-intake timing, activity demands, symptom development and recovery, together with relevant health and medication context.
- **System relationship:** Consider nutritional, endocrine, circulatory and other contributors on their own evidence. State which metabolic–autonomic relationship is proposed.

### Lower Urinary Tract and Sexual Function

- Consider whether autonomic regulation of storage, emptying, genital vascular responses or secretion could explain or contribute to the symptoms.
- Relate the proposed function to relevant central, pelvic autonomic and peripheral pathways, alongside somatic sensory and motor control.
- Interpret pelvic complaints through the interaction of organ function, neural regulation and pelvic-floor function. Urinary, bowel and sexual history may provide relevant context for one another. [EAU Neuro-urology guideline](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/neuro-urology/chapter/the-guideline)

- **Possible symptoms and findings:** Changes in urinary storage or emptying and sexual function, with or without pelvic discomfort.
- **History pattern:** Explore relationships to filling, voiding, sexual activity, onset/course and relevant neurological, surgical or medication history.
- **System relationship:** Keep autonomic regulation, pelvic-floor activity, somatic neural function and local organ explanations separately supported. Link bowel-related explanations to the gastrointestinal direction where relevant.

### Cranial Autonomic and Ocular Function

- Consider whether cranial autonomic reflexes, glandular secretion, pupillary control or accommodation could contribute to the symptoms.
- For headache or facial pain with eye, nasal or facial changes, examine their laterality, timing and relationship to pain. Relevant mechanisms can include trigeminal–autonomic reflex relationships. [IHS classification and physiological discussion](https://ichd-3.org/3-trigeminal-autonomic-cephalalgias/)
- Select the cranial sympathetic, parasympathetic, sensory and effector relationships relevant to the complaint.

- **Possible symptoms and findings:** Head or facial pain accompanied by tearing, eye redness, nasal changes or facial sweating; difficulty adapting to light or focusing; relevant pupil or eyelid changes.
- **History pattern:** Clarify whether features precede, accompany or persist beyond pain, and whether they occur on the same side.
- **System relationship:** Distinguish ocular autonomic functions from other visual, oculomotor, sensory and vestibular processes. A visual complaint may require several explanatory directions.

### Airway Autonomic Regulation

- Consider whether autonomic control of airway smooth muscle or secretion could contribute to the presentation.
- Relate the proposed process to the affected airway function and relevant sensory–autonomic reflex relationships.
- Human airway autonomic regulation includes prominent parasympathetic effects on smooth muscle and glands. Respiratory rhythm, respiratory muscle control and airway autonomic function represent different explanatory processes. [Human airway innervation and function](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10213912/)

- **Possible symptoms and findings:** Breathing discomfort, chest tightness, cough or secretion-related difficulty.
- **History pattern:** Explore relationships to exertion, environmental exposure, posture and associated airway symptoms.
- **System relationship:** Consider respiratory, cardiovascular, sensory, mechanical and contextual explanations according to the presentation. Specify the proposed autonomic link.

## Contextual Clues and Functional Relationships

- Use muscle tension, stiffness, perceived weakness, sensory changes, swelling, fatigue, sleep disturbance and stress- or breathing-related symptom variation as contextual clues when exploring autonomic contributions.
- Connect each relevant clue to a proposed autonomic function, effector or integrated response. When the relationship is unclear, identify what requires clarification.
- Describe whether the clue concerns symptom generation, symptom modulation, activity tolerance, recovery or an accompanying response.
- Consider relevant neural, vascular, lymphatic, musculoskeletal, visceral, endocrine and contextual explanations alongside the autonomic candidate.
- Preserve symptom IDs and independently varying complaints. Exploring a relationship does not require concluding that symptoms share one source during subjective Phase 1.

## Pain–Autonomic Relationships

Consider the following relationships across the explanatory directions:

1. **Pain-evoked responses:** Nociceptive input and pain can evoke autonomic responses. Interpret these as possible accompanying reactions unless additional evidence supports another role.
2. **Peripheral contributions:** Autonomic effects on vessels, glands or the local tissue environment may contribute to symptoms. Identify the intervening process.
3. **Nociceptive modulation:** Adrenergic influences on nociception may be relevant in particular sensitized conditions. Human experimental evidence supports this possibility under specific conditions; establish its relevance separately in the case. [Schattschneider et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17234439/)
4. **Shared regulation:** Pain and autonomic responses may change together through shared central integration or context. Their association alone leaves the causal direction unresolved.

Human experimental pain can produce differing sympathetic responses, supporting attention to the specific function and relationship rather than assuming uniform sympathetic activation. [Kobuch et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29541532/)

Keep pain descriptors and neural mechanosensitivity assessment under the shared [pain-mechanism guide](pain-mechanisms.md).

## State the Candidate and Its Basis

For each selected explanation, record:

1. **Candidate:** The proposed autonomic function or regulatory relationship.
2. **Case basis:** The symptom features and context that make it worth considering.
3. **Mechanistic connection:** How the proposed function could explain or contribute to the symptom.
4. **Structures and regions:** Relevant pathways, effectors and anatomical regions, at the level supported by the information.
5. **Uncertainty:** Unestablished links, competing explanations and information that could change the hypothesis.

Anatomical continuity supports exploration of a relationship; patient-specific dysfunction requires its own evidence.

## Verification of the Proposed Relationship

Select verification according to the proposed relationship:

- **Phenomenon:** Clarify whether the reported sensation corresponds to an observable or measurable change, preserving symptom state and assessment conditions.
- **Functional relationship:** Examine whether the proposed process, symptom timing and affected region correspond.
- **Alternative explanations:** Obtain information that distinguishes the autonomic contribution from other relevant processes.
- **Autonomic function:** When needed, select an appropriate clinical screen or specialist investigation for the particular function and population.
- **Hypothesis update:** Explain which findings support, weaken or redirect the candidate, and which links remain unresolved.

Match conclusions to the function and circumstances assessed. Standardized autonomic investigations examine defined physiological domains; their interpretation requires suitable methods, reference data and clinical context. [Cheshire et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33419664/)

A symptom change after breathing, posture modification or another intervention demonstrates a response to that condition. Identify the additional evidence needed to attribute the response to an autonomic mechanism.

For subjective Table 1.2, use the three columns as follows:

- **Explanation & Basis:** Summarize the proposed function or regulatory relationship, its possible symptom role, case basis and material uncertainty.
- **Structures & Relationships:** Record relevant pathways, effectors, anatomical regions and their proposed relationships. Describe directional pathway or upstream/downstream relationships when relevant to the candidate, and network or regulatory relationships as appropriate; select the anatomy relevant to the explanation rather than reproducing the entire autonomic anatomy.

Maintain the following responsibilities:

- **Nervous system:** Neural pathways, sensory and motor function, neural mechanical function and relevant neural processes.
- **Vessels:** Perfusion, vascular regulation, venous return and pressure.
- **ANS:** The proposed autonomic regulation of relevant functions and effectors.
- **Lymphatic and other systems:** Their own transport, organ or physiological processes, connected to the autonomic candidate only where justified.

When detail is needed, use an optional system expansion under the subjective skill's output rules. ## Medical-Assessment Priority

Apply the [clinical contract](clinical-contract.md) immediately when potentially urgent features appear.

- Sudden limb pain or coolness with new sensory loss or weakness, or new unexplained unilateral swelling, requires prioritization under the [vascular guidance](vascular-analysis.md).
- Severe chest symptoms, acute breathlessness, collapse or marked clinical instability require immediate medical assessment.
- New bladder, bowel or sexual dysfunction with back/leg symptoms, perineal sensory change or progressive neurological deficits requires immediate assessment. [NICE neurological referral guidance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng127/chapter/Recommendations-for-adults-aged-over-16)
- Sudden severe headache, new neurological findings, acute pupil changes or painful visual disturbance warrants urgent assessment according to the presentation. [NICE headache guidance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg150/chapter/Recommendations), [NSW ophthalmic emergency guidance](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/outpatients/referrals/Pages/ophthalmic-emergencies.aspx)
- Heat-related deterioration with confusion, collapse or altered consciousness requires emergency assessment; sweating may be present or absent. [CDC heat-related illness guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/heat-stress/about/illnesses.html)

Persistent or progressive unexplained autonomic-related functional changes warrant appropriate medical evaluation even without immediate instability.

## Sources and Limits

- Established physiology provides candidate mechanisms; it does not establish their contribution in an individual.
- Experimental findings retain their original tissue, stimulus, population and methodological limits.
- The explanatory directions and workflow are project clinical-reasoning guidance, not a validated diagnostic algorithm.
- Consult current authoritative sources when a specific mechanism, assessment method or medical priority requires clarification.
- Preserve source-specific uncertainty and distinguish patient findings from reference-based knowledge.
