# Vascular analysis for clinical reasoning

## Purpose

- **Scope:** Explore how arterial perfusion, venous return and vascular regulation could explain or contribute to each symptom.
- **Table distinction:** Table 1.1 identifies specific adjacent and remote vessel-source candidates. Table 1.2 integrates relevant candidates into circulatory processes and regional relationships.
- **Selection:** Use case-supported directions below, not a mandatory disease checklist. Symptoms such as pain, coldness, numbness, swelling, heaviness or color change are clues, not vascular diagnoses.
- **Broader context:** When indicated, add separate system/disease candidates for cardiac output, systemic blood pressure or oxygen-carrying problems rather than forcing them into a local vessel explanation.

## Step 1: Define the Symptom and Context

1. **Symptom scope:** Preserve the symptom ID, distribution, onset/course and associated symptoms. Distinguish sudden changes from longstanding or recurrent patterns.
2. **Provocation and recovery:** Clarify relationships to exertion, sustained posture, limb position, temperature and emotional stress, including onset and recovery timing.
3. **Reported versus observed:** Distinguish perceived coldness or swelling from measured temperature differences or observed edema. Preserve patient descriptions without treating them as examination findings.
4. **Relevant history:** Ask about vascular disease, thrombosis, diabetes, smoking, recent surgery or immobilization, cancer, medications and other risks only as relevant to the presentation.
5. **Source candidates:** Review Table 1.1's adjacent and remote vascular candidates and identify gaps that could change the proposed process.

## Step 2: Select Relevant Explanatory Directions

### Arterial Perfusion

- Consider whether blood delivery could be insufficient for tissue demand, including activity-related symptoms or more severe resting symptoms and impaired healing.
- Explore plausible narrowing, occlusion or other arterial processes without assuming atherosclerosis in every case.
- Vascular insufficiency may contribute to numbness or weakness as well as pain; vascular and neural explanations are not mutually exclusive.
- Distinguish possible impaired perfusion from an established vascular lesion and from proof that the lesion causes the complaint.

- **Possible symptoms and findings:** Pain or cramping, heaviness or fatigue with activity, coldness, numbness, tingling or weakness. More severe presentations may include resting pain, impaired wound healing or tissue loss.
- **History pattern:** Clarify the activity required to provoke symptoms, their location, recovery after stopping, resting symptoms and relevant vascular risks.

### Vascular Regulation

- Consider episodic vasoconstriction when symptoms have a relevant temperature/stress relationship, particularly with digital color changes and recovery.
- Reduced perfusion is an outcome; fixed vascular disease and dynamic vasoconstriction are possible mechanisms and may coexist.
- A Raynaud-type pattern warrants assessment of the vascular phenomenon and relevant underlying disease.
- Coldness, stress sensitivity or improvement with warming alone does not establish pathological ischemia or ANS dysfunction.

- **Possible symptoms and findings:** Digital coldness, numbness, pain and pallor or blue discoloration; returning blood flow may be accompanied by redness, tingling, burning, throbbing or swelling. The combination and sequence vary.
- **History pattern:** Clarify cold or emotional triggers, episode duration, affected digits, recovery with warming and associated health conditions.
- **System relationship:** Describe the vascular phenomenon first, then consider neural/ANS regulation and local vascular factors according to the available evidence.

### Venous Return and Pressure

- Consider reflux, outflow obstruction and impaired muscle-pump function when supported by swelling, heaviness, aching, skin changes or positional behavior.
- Distinguish chronic venous patterns from possible acute thrombosis.
- Reduced walking, ankle movement or calf-pump activity may be relevant contributors, but do not establish venous disease or explain all swelling by themselves.
- Specify the suspected process and relevant drainage pathway rather than listing veins without a proposed relationship.

- **Possible symptoms and findings:** Aching, heaviness, swelling or a sensation of swelling, burning and itching; persistent disease may involve skin discoloration, skin changes or ulceration.
- **History pattern:** Explore relationships to standing, sitting, walking, limb elevation and time of day, together with previous thrombosis and changes in mobility.
- **Functional contribution:** Consider calf-pump activity and ankle movement as potential influences on venous return, alongside vessel and valve function.

### Venous Thrombosis

- Consider thrombotic obstruction of venous flow when the presentation and risk context make it relevant.
- Identify the suspected venous territory and distinguish a current thrombotic candidate from a history of previous thrombosis.

- **Possible symptoms and findings:** New limb swelling, pain or tenderness, warmth and discoloration, with variable combinations.
- **History pattern:** Clarify onset, progression, distribution, previous thrombosis and relevant recent events or risks. Identify associated breathlessness, chest pain or fainting for immediate medical prioritization.

### Edema and Overlapping Systems

- Swelling does not by itself establish venous disease or lymphedema.
- Consider venous pressure, lymphatic transport, local inflammation and systemic contributors according to history and distribution.
- Venous and lymphatic problems may coexist; persistent increased fluid load can be accompanied by reduced lymphatic transport capacity.
- Pitting or improvement with elevation alone does not reliably separate venous from lymphatic edema.
- Expand relevant system/disease candidates without requiring a decision about coexistence or causation during subjective Phase 1.

- **Possible symptoms and findings:** Swelling, tightness, heaviness, discomfort and changes in tissue or skin characteristics.
- **History pattern:** Clarify distribution, onset, daily variation, positional response and relevant local or systemic history.
- **System relationship:** Explore venous, lymphatic and other candidates according to the presentation, preserving the possibility of overlapping contributions.

## Step 3: State the Candidate and Its Basis

For each selected explanation, record:

1. **Candidate:** The proposed perfusion, return or regulation problem.
2. **Case basis:** Relevant symptom behavior, distribution and history.
3. **Structures and regions:** The suspected supply/drainage pathway and affected territory, including proximal structures when justified.
4. **Uncertainty:** Missing or inconsistent information and the assessment that could support, weaken or change the explanation.

For a proposed interaction with nerves, ANS or lymphatics, state the connection and its evidence rather than automatically marking every related system as involved.

## Step 4: Select Verification for the Proposed Process

Select assessments that answer the candidate's clinical question. Match the method to the affected region, patient context and applicable guidance, and explain how the result would update the hypothesis.

- **Arterial perfusion:** Assess whether blood delivery is adequate at rest and during symptom-provoking activity. Integrate pulses, skin/wound findings and appropriate physiological testing. For lower-extremity PAD, use guideline-directed resting ABI, exercise ABI or toe-pressure/TBI assessment as indicated.

- **Vascular regulation:** Characterize the episodes through trigger, distribution, color/temperature changes and recovery. For a suspected Raynaud pattern, use clinical assessment and, when indicated, nailfold capillaroscopy or targeted investigations for associated disease.

- **Venous return and pressure:** Assess edema, skin changes, positional behavior and relevant movement or muscle-pump function. Use venous duplex to investigate suspected reflux or obstruction and relate the findings to the symptom pattern.

- **Venous thrombosis:** Arrange timely medical assessment using a validated diagnostic pathway. Combine clinical probability with indicated D-dimer and ultrasound testing, following the appropriate branch and follow-up of the pathway.

- **Edema and overlapping systems:** Document distribution, reproducible limb measurements, tissue/skin findings and positional response. Use the history and examination to select further vascular, lymphatic or systemic assessment.

- **Integrated functions or Systems:** Retain the Vessels row label.

- **Regions This System Passes Through:** Record the relevant supply/drainage territories and regional relationship, not every region traversed by the circulation.

## Medical-Assessment Priority

- Sudden pain, pallor or coolness with new sensory loss or weakness warrants emergency assessment for possible acute limb ischemia.
- New unexplained unilateral swelling and pain with relevant thrombotic risk warrants urgent medical assessment for possible DVT.
- Suspected PE symptoms, including unexplained acute breathlessness, chest pain or syncope, require emergency assessment.
- Persistent ischemic rest pain, nonhealing wounds or tissue loss warrants prompt vascular assessment.

Apply the [clinical contract](clinical-contract.md) immediately; do not wait for table completion.

## Sources and Limits

- [2024 ACC/AHA lower-extremity PAD guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/full/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001251): lower-extremity arterial assessment, physiological testing and medical-assessment priorities; recommendations retain their regional and population limits.
- [NHLBI PAD symptoms](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/peripheral-artery-disease/symptoms): symptom patterns associated with peripheral arterial disease.
- [ESVS 2022 chronic venous disease guideline](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/312766110/1_s2.0_S1078588421009795_main.pdf): lower-limb venous processes, symptoms, muscle-pump contributions and duplex assessment; functional differences require clinical correspondence.
- [NICE NG158](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng158/chapter/Recommendations): diagnostic pathways for suspected DVT and PE; use the applicable population, probability assessment and follow-up.
- [CDC VTE overview](https://www.cdc.gov/blood-clots/about/): presenting symptoms and urgency of suspected DVT/PE.
- [NIAMS Raynaud phenomenon](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/raynauds-phenomenon) and [diagnostic guidance](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/raynauds-phenomenon/diagnosis-treatment-and-steps-to-take): episode patterns and assessment for associated disease; not a general diagnostic framework for ANS dysfunction.
- [ISL 2023 consensus](https://isl.arizona.edu/sites/default/files/2024-11/THE-DIAGNOSIS-AND-TREATMENT-OF-PERIPHERAL-LYMPHEDEMA-2023-CONSENSUS-DOCUMENT-OF-THE-INTERNATIONAL-SOCIETY-OF-LYMPHOLOGY.pdf): lymphatic transport, edema differentiation and overlapping contributions; consensus recommendations reflect varying evidence levels.

The table responsibilities and workflow above are project reasoning guidance, not a validated diagnostic algorithm. Source-specific recommendations retain their original population and regional limits.
